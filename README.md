Why Warehouse Planning Begins With Operations

Before discussing steel, roofing or concrete, define what the building must do.

A warehouse should be designed around its operational workflow.

For example:

Inbound vehicles

↓

Gate

↓

Security

↓

Unloading

↓

Receiving

↓

Inspection

↓

Storage

↓

Picking

↓

Packing

↓

Dispatch

↓

Outbound vehicles

Every transition creates a spatial requirement.

If trucks cannot manoeuvre efficiently, the site becomes inefficient.

If docks are poorly positioned, loading time increases.

If columns interfere with racking, usable storage volume decreases.

If clear heights are inadequate, vertical storage potential is lost.

If the floor is not designed for the intended equipment, operational problems can emerge after handover.

Warehouse construction should therefore begin with the operational model.

3. Clear Height and Storage Volume

Floor area is only one part of warehouse capacity.

Vertical volume can be equally important.

Higher clear heights can support:

- Multi-level racking
- Pallet storage
- Automated storage
- Bulk storage
- Mezzanine systems
- Conveyor systems

However, increasing building height can also influence:

- Structural design
- Wind loads
- Fire protection
- Lighting
- Ventilation
- Construction cost
- Maintenance

The correct clear height should therefore be established from the storage strategy rather than selected arbitrarily.

---

4. Column Grid and Structural Planning

The structural grid directly affects warehouse operations.

A poorly selected column arrangement can interfere with:

- Racking
- Forklift movement
- Conveyors
- Storage aisles
- Loading areas
- Production lines

A well-coordinated structural system should balance:

span + column spacing + roof loads + storage requirements + equipment movement + construction economics

For large facilities, column-free or wide-span areas can provide significant operational flexibility, but the structural solution should be engineered around the actual project loads.

6. Foundations and Geotechnical Conditions

26. Common Warehouse Planning Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Designing only for floor area

Storage capacity depends on volume and operational efficiency, not floor area alone.

Mistake 2 — Ignoring truck circulation

A large building can still operate poorly if vehicles cannot manoeuvre efficiently.

Mistake 3 — Choosing the structural grid too late

Racking and equipment can conflict with columns.

Mistake 4 — Treating fire protection as an afterthought

Fire systems should be integrated into the building design.

Mistake 5 — Underestimating procurement

Steel, roofing, equipment and specialist systems can have different lead times.

Mistake 6 — Comparing quotations without normalizing scope

A lower number may simply exclude important packages.

Mistake 7 — Ignoring future expansion

Today's efficient warehouse can become tomorrow's constraint.

28. Choosing the Right Warehouse Construction Model

There are several possible approaches.

Traditional multi-contractor model

Separate companies handle civil, steel, electrical, fire and other packages.

Potential advantage: specialist procurement flexibility.

Potential challenge: greater coordination responsibility.

Design-and-build

A single organization coordinates design and construction.

Potential advantage: fewer interfaces.

Potential challenge: scope and design responsibilities must be clearly defined.

Turnkey EPC-style approach

A broader single-point responsibility model can coordinate engineering, procurement and construction.

Potential advantage: integrated project management.

Potential challenge: contract scope, performance obligations and exclusions must be carefully documented.

The appropriate model depends on the owner's internal project-management capability.

The Real Economics of Warehouse Construction

The cheapest warehouse is not necessarily the most economical warehouse.

A lower initial construction cost can become expensive if it produces:

- Poor storage density
- Excessive forklift travel
- Loading delays
- High energy consumption
- Maintenance problems
- Difficult expansion
- Poor fire protection
- Repeated repairs
- Operational bottlenecks

A better approach evaluates:

construction cost + operating efficiency + maintenance + flexibility + lifespan

The building is an operational asset.

Its design should therefore support the business model that will occupy it.
